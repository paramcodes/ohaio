## The short definition

Progressive disclosure is a decision about what to withhold. The common version — collapse advanced settings behind "More options" — is the visible half. The invisible half is that the moment you hide something, you have chosen *how* it stops existing, and the platform has five different answers to that question. They do not behave the same way.

The technique itself is old and well-supported. Deferring advanced or rarely used features to a secondary surface measurably improves learnability, efficiency, and error rate, because the first screen asks for less. That part is settled. What breaks in practice is the mechanism underneath it: a disclosure that hides the right pixels while leaving the controls reachable is not a disclosure at all. It is an invisible trap that happens to be tabbable.

## Hiding is not one property

Every one of these removes content from view. Measured on this page's lab — a revealed panel holding one text input and one button, collapsed by each method in turn — they differ on two axes that matter: does it still take up space, and can a keyboard user still reach it.

| Collapse method | Panel height | Control focusable | Perceivable | Where Tab from the trigger lands |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `display: none` | `0 px` | no | no | the control after the panel |
| `visibility: hidden` | `137 px` | no | no | the control after the panel |
| `opacity: 0` | `137 px` | **yes** | no | **inside the hidden panel** |
| `hidden` attribute | `0 px` | no | no | the control after the panel |
| `inert` attribute | `137 px` | no | yes | the control after the panel |

Three facts fall out of that table, and none of them are obvious from the property names:

- **`display: none` and `visibility: hidden` both remove content from the accessibility tree and from the tab order.** They differ only in layout: `display: none` collapses the box to zero height, `visibility: hidden` keeps the space reserved. MDN is explicit that `visibility: hidden` "remains invisible but still affects layout."
- **`opacity: 0` does neither.** It is a paint operation. The element keeps its box, stays in the accessibility tree, and stays focusable. A keyboard user tabs straight into controls they cannot see.
- **`inert` is the odd one out: it removes interactivity while keeping the content fully visible.** It is the only method in the table whose "Perceivable" column reads `yes`.

## The trap: a fade that stops at transparent

The `opacity: 0` row is not a theoretical footgun. It is what happens when you animate a disclosure closed and forget the last step.

A transition can only animate properties that have intermediate values. `display` has none — it flips. So the standard recipe for an animated collapse is to fade `opacity` (and often `height` or `transform`), because those interpolate smoothly. But a fade ends at `opacity: 0`, and `opacity: 0` still leaves the subtree focusable and hit-testable. You have animated the content out of *sight* without animating it out of *existence*.

The fix is to make the invisible state also the inert state once the transition finishes:

```css
.panel {
  opacity: 1;
  visibility: visible;
  transition:
    opacity 180ms ease,
    visibility 180ms; /* not interpolated — it flips at the end */
}

.panel[data-open="false"] {
  opacity: 0;
  visibility: hidden; /* takes it out of the tab order when the fade lands */
}
```

`visibility` is the trick: unlike `display`, it participates in a transition, but instead of interpolating it waits until the end of the duration to flip. So the panel fades out over 180 ms and only *then* leaves the accessibility tree and the tab order. On the way back, `visibility` flips to `visible` at the very start, so the fade is seen. The same effect is available with `transition-behavior: allow-discrete`, which lets `display: none` itself hold until the end of a transition — the modern way to animate an element in and out without a JS timer.

## The browser already ships one

Before reaching for a button and a hidden div, note that HTML has had a disclosure widget since 2012:

<details class="pd-example">
  <summary>What a closed <code>&lt;details&gt;</code> actually does</summary>
  <div class="pd-example-body">
    <p>The content after the <code>&lt;summary&gt;</code> is rendered inside a <code>::details-content</code> pseudo-element that the UA gives <code>content-visibility: hidden</code> while the element is closed. That is a third hiding mode, distinct from all five in the table: the DOM is fully present, but the content is not laid out, not painted, and not focusable.</p>
    <p class="pd-example-probe">Focus this field while the box is closed, then open it and try again: <input class="pd-example-input" type="text" data-probe placeholder="a control inside the details" /></p>
  </div>
</details>

Measured on this page: with the example closed, the whole `<details>` is `47 px` tall and its inner `<input>` is in the DOM (`querySelector` finds it) but refuses focus — `element.focus()` leaves `document.activeElement` unchanged. Open it and the box grows to `203 px` and the same field takes focus. The content was always there; only the open state lets you reach it. This is why `<details>` is the right default for accordions and FAQ rows — you get correct hiding semantics, keyboard support, and an accessible name for the toggle for free, with no script.

The trade-offs are real, though. The toggle is a `<summary>`, not a `<button>`, so its styling and ARIA shape differ from the rest of your controls; the open/closed state is not React state unless you mirror it; and animating the open is awkward precisely because `content-visibility` is discrete (which `transition-behavior: allow-discrete` and the `::details-content` pseudo-element exist to fix).

## The disclosure contract

When you build the widget yourself, the WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices disclosure pattern is prescriptive about three attributes, and the lab's toggle obeys all of them:

- The thing that shows and hides the content is a **`<button>`** (or `role="button"`), not a `<div>` or a link.
- When the content is visible, that button carries **`aria-expanded="true"`**; when hidden, `aria-expanded="false"`.
- The button names the region it controls with **`aria-controls`**, so a screen-reader user at the toggle can find out what it will reveal.

`aria-expanded` describes the *button*; the *region* it reveals should be taken out of the tree by the hiding method, not by slapping `aria-hidden="true"` on it. `aria-hidden` is a lie if the content is still focusable — and a redundancy if you already used `display: none`. Reach for the hiding mechanism, not the ARIA, to remove content. This is the same mistake, wearing a different hat, as the `opacity: 0` trap: both hide the wrong layer.

## When to withhold

NN/g's framing is that progressive disclosure improves learnability, efficiency of use, and error rate by reducing the options on the primary screen — but it makes the hidden options harder to *discover*. That tension is the decision:

- **Good to disclose:** rarely used or expert-only settings; anything that would overwhelm a first-time user; detail that only matters once a prior choice is made (conditional fields); long reference text.
- **Bad to disclose:** anything the majority of users need on first visit; a step in a task they are mid-way through; an error message; a primary action. Hiding a required field behind "advanced" does not reduce its importance, only the chance it gets filled in.

The failure mode of the *technique* is over-disclosure — burying the thing everyone needs. The failure mode of the *implementation* is a hidden-but-reachable trap. Both are worth checking for, and only the second one is measurable with `getBoundingClientRect` and a Tab key.

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| Keyboard focus lands on invisible controls | Collapsed with `opacity: 0` (or `transform: scale(0)`) and nothing else |
| The layout jumps when a panel opens | The panel was `display: none`, so its space appeared with it; reserve it with `visibility: hidden` or a grid row if the jump hurts |
| Screen reader announces content you cannot see | `opacity: 0` / `height: 0; overflow: hidden` without `visibility: hidden` — still in the a11y tree |
| `aria-hidden` content still focusable | `aria-hidden` hides from AT but not from the keyboard; the controls need `inert` or a real hide |
| The disclosure cannot be reached by keyboard at all | Toggle built as a `<div>` or `<a href="#">` instead of a `<button>` |
| Focus is lost when a panel closes | The focused element was inside the panel that just left the DOM/tree; move focus back to the toggle |
| An animated open does nothing | `display` has no intermediate value; needs `transition-behavior: allow-discrete` or `@starting-style` |

## Distinctions

- **Progressive disclosure vs. chunking / steppers.** A stepper splits one task into ordered steps and shows progress; every step is mandatory. Progressive disclosure hides *optional* depth on a single screen. If the user must pass through it, it is a step, not a disclosure.
- **Progressive disclosure vs. a tooltip or hovercard.** A tooltip shows the same information to everyone, non-interactively, on hover. A disclosure reveals *more* information that the user asked for and can usually act on.
- **Progressive disclosure vs. pagination / lazy loading.** Those hide content because there is too much of it to move at once (a data-volume decision). Disclosure hides content because most of it is not relevant right now (an attention decision).

## Check yourself

1. You animate a settings panel closed with `opacity: 0` over 200 ms and it looks perfect. What is still true about its inputs during and after the fade, and what one property fixes it without a timer?
2. A collapsed `<details>` element: is its inner `<input>` present in the DOM? Is it focusable? Which of those two answers surprises people, and why?
3. Your toggle sets `aria-expanded="false"` and adds `aria-hidden="true"` to the panel, but a keyboard tester still tabs through it. What did `aria-hidden` fail to do, and what attribute or property would actually remove the panel from the tab order?
